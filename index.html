Quantifying event contracts

We price resolution risk to make prediction markets bankable

10bips team, imagined in Poland

Abstract. Before a prediction market pays, someone has to report what happened, and that report can be disputed, reversed or paid late. No one prices this resolution risk today, so a position is hardest to value, and to lend against, in the hours before it pays. We estimate it for every outcome proposed on Polymarket, at the moment it is proposed, and quote it in basis points of notional. Each day's estimates are frozen with a public hash before the outcomes are known, so anyone can check the record.

i. The problem

Every event contract pays 1 or 0 once someone decides what happened. That decision can be disputed, reversed or paid late. 10bips puts a price on it, per proposal, the moment it lands.

Near its end, a winning position is the safest asset in the market: the outcome is public and the price sits close to $1. It is also the one point where a single dispute can send it to zero, or lock the capital for days while the dispute is settled. The price shows neither risk.

This now has a cost. Venues and lenders are adding margin and credit to prediction markets, and a lender has to value a position exactly when it nears settlement. Without a price for resolution risk, the safe answer is to stop lending as settlement approaches, so capital that could be working waits until the market pays. When a large market is disputed in public, the cost is trust in every other market too.

ii. Resolution, live

A Polymarket market resolves in three steps. Someone proposes the outcome. A two-hour window opens in which anyone can dispute it by posting a bond. If no one does, the market settles. Table 1 shows these steps as they happen, read from public on-chain data.

iii. What we measure

P(dispute)

The probability that a proposal is disputed within its two-hour window. Estimated when the proposal is made, from information public at that moment.

Resolution risk

The expected loss to a holder of the proposed outcome, in basis points of notional: the probability of a dispute multiplied by what a dispute costs. The cost has two parts, the chance that the payout changes and the capital locked while the dispute is settled.

Haircut

The discount a lender should apply to a position before it settles. Resolution risk is an average; the haircut is set by the bad cases, how far a position can fall when a dispute goes wrong.

iv. Method

Resolution risk is an expected loss. Follow one proposal from start to payment; each step adds a term to equation (1).

D is a dispute and Δ a change in payout. L is the loss per $1 if the payout changes. τ is the time from dispute to settlement, and r the yearly cost of the capital locked during it. The factor 10⁴ turns the result into basis points.

Polymarket resolves markets through UMA's optimistic oracle, in stages that are easy to mistake for a single event [1]. A proposer posts a bond and an outcome. If the two-hour window passes without a dispute, the outcome is final. A first dispute sends the question back for a new proposal and a new window. A second dispute goes to a vote of UMA token holders, which follows 1 to 2 days of public debate [5] and returns one of four answers: the proposer was right, the disputer was right, the question was asked too early, or the payout is split 50/50. Every path after a dispute changes what the market pays, when it pays, or both.

Estimating each term

P(D)

A statistical model trained on every past proposal and whether it was disputed. It uses only what is public when the proposal is made: who proposed it and how their earlier proposals ended, how soon after the question opened the proposal came, how the proposed outcome compares with the last market price, and the type of market. The wording of the rules is a weak signal on its own [4], which is why the model leans on who proposes and when. Its probabilities are calibrated, so proposals scored at 1% are disputed about 1% of the time.

P(Δ | D)

The share of past disputes, by type of market, in which the final payout differed from the first proposal.

L

1 for someone who holds to settlement: the proposed side pays nothing if the payout changes. For a lender, the gap between the collateral value and what the position finally pays.

E[τ | D]

The expected time from dispute to settlement, measured on past disputes along each path in Figure 2, steps 3 to 5.

r

The cost of locked capital, set by whoever uses the number; 5% a year by default. Markets already discount near-certain contracts for this lock-up [2].

Haircut

Not an average. Taken from the distribution of how far the proposed side fell after past disputes, by price level and position size, at a high percentile.

How it is checked

  1. 1Out of time. The model is trained on older proposals and tested only on newer ones it has never seen, the way it is used live.
  2. 2On the rare cases. Disputes are rare, so a model that always answers "unlikely" looks accurate and is useless. We judge it by how many real disputes fall among the proposals it ranks as riskiest.
  3. 3Frozen daily. Each day's estimates are written to a file, and the file's SHA-256 hash is published before the outcomes are known. Anyone can later confirm that no estimate was changed.
  4. 4Misses included. Every dispute is compared with the number published before it, and misses are published in the same format as hits.

Limits

  1. 1Once a dispute is filed, traders price the likely verdict of the vote [3], and the market price becomes the best forecast of the verdict within minutes. Our number covers the period before a dispute; the verdict itself is left to the market.
  2. 2The rarest cases are the hardest to estimate. We publish how well the number holds at each level of risk, including where it holds least.
